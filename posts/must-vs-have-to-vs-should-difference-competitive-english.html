"Must," "have to," and "should" all get translated the same way in most students' heads — as "obligation." That single mental shortcut is exactly what error-spotting and sentence-improvement questions in SSC CGL and Bank PO are built to catch. These three words don't carry the same weight: one is a personal judgment, one is an external rule, and one is only advice. Textbooks list their meanings side by side and move on, but exams test the boundary between them — and that boundary is where marks quietly disappear.

Quick Answer: "Must" expresses the speaker's own strong opinion or a logical conclusion. "Have to" expresses an obligation imposed by an outside rule, authority, or circumstance. "Should" expresses advice or a mild recommendation, not a firm requirement. All three connect to the base form of the verb, and only "have to" changes form for tense and subject ("has to," "had to").

Why Must, Have To, and Should Feel Harder Than They Are

In casual speech, these three words get used loosely and nobody notices. In a formal, exam-graded sentence, that looseness is exactly what gets tested. Here's where the confusion usually starts:

  • Students learn all three as "obligation" without learning that the strength of that obligation is different for each one.
  • "Have to" is the only one of the three that changes form ("has to," "had to"), and that grammatical detail gets ignored in favor of memorizing meaning alone.
  • "Should" gets used interchangeably with "must," even though "should" is genuinely optional advice, not a requirement.
  • Negative forms are especially tricky — "must not" (prohibition) and "don't have to" (no obligation) look similar but mean almost opposite things.
  • Students who use these words correctly in daily conversation still hesitate the moment a sentence isolates just one of them for correction.

After years of teaching this exact comparison, one thing is consistent with every batch: students already understand these words individually. What trips them up is choosing the right one between two options that both sound grammatically fine — which is precisely the skill sentence-improvement and error-spotting questions are designed to test.

Common Mistakes Students Make

Mistake 1: Treating "must" and "have to" as always identical. Both suggest obligation, but "must" reflects the speaker's personal judgment, while "have to" reflects a rule or requirement coming from outside the speaker.

Mistake 2: Forgetting that "have to" changes form. Unlike "must," which never changes, "have to" becomes "has to" with third-person singular subjects and "had to" in the past.

Mistake 3: Confusing "must not" with "don't have to." "Must not" means something is forbidden. "Don't have to" means something is optional. Mixing these up completely reverses the sentence's meaning.

✅ Correct: You don't have to worry about the exact seating arrangement, it isn't compulsory.

The Complete System (Step-by-Step)

Instead of treating these as three shades of the same meaning, treat them as three different sources of obligation: "must" comes from the speaker, "have to" comes from an outside rule, and "should" isn't obligation at all — it's advice.

WordSource of ObligationExample
MustSpeaker's own strong opinion or logical conclusionYou must double-check your OMR sheet before submitting.
Have ToExternal rule, requirement, or circumstanceCandidates have to reach the exam center by 9 AM sharp.
ShouldAdvice or recommendation, not a firm ruleYou should revise grammar rules a day before the exam.

Step 1: Ask where the obligation is coming from. If it's the speaker's own conviction, use "must." If it's an external rule, use "have to."

Rule + Example: "I must finish this chapter tonight" (personal resolve) vs. "Students have to wear their ID cards inside the exam hall" (external rule).

Step 2: Remember "have to" is the only one that conjugates. "Have to" becomes "has to" for he/she/it, and "had to" in the past. "Must" and "should" never change form.

Step 3: Treat "should" as advice, not requirement. If a sentence could reasonably be ignored without breaking a rule, "should" is usually the correct choice, not "must."

Rule + Example: "You should attempt the easier questions first" is advice — skipping it isn't wrong, just less strategic. "You must sign the attendance sheet" is a firm requirement.

Step 4: Separate "must not" from "don't have to" carefully. "Must not" forbids an action. "Don't have to" simply removes the obligation — the action is still allowed, just optional.

Step 5: Use "should have" for missed advice in the past, not "must have." "You should have revised this topic" expresses regret over a missed recommendation, while "must have" expresses a logical deduction ("He must have left already").

Step 6: Never combine two of these in a row. "Must should," "have to must" — none of these exist in standard English, however natural they might sound when rushed.

Step-by-Step Strategy

1

Identify which of the three words is used before judging the rest of the sentence.

2

Ask whether the obligation comes from the speaker or from an outside rule.

3

If it's advice rather than a rule, switch to "should" instead of "must."

4

If "have to" is used, check subject-verb agreement — "has to" or "had to" as needed.

5

For negatives, confirm whether the sentence needs prohibition ("must not") or optionality ("don't have to").

6

Scan for two of these words stacked together — that combination is always incorrect.

A Railway exam aspirant in one of my batches consistently missed sentence-improvement questions involving "must not" and "don't have to," always assuming both meant roughly the same thing. Once we worked through a handful of side-by-side examples — sentences where swapping one for the other completely reversed the meaning — the distinction finally clicked. Expert Tips From the Classroom

  • Ask "whose rule is this?" If it's the speaker's own opinion, it's "must." If it's an institution's rule, it's "have to."
  • Treat "should" as removable. If the sentence would still make sense without the obligation, "should" is likely the right word, not "must."
  • Double-check "have to" for subject agreement. It's the only one of the three that changes with person and tense — an easy detail to miss under time pressure.
  • Never assume negatives mirror positives. "Must" and "must not" both express strong statements, but "have to" and "don't have to" point in almost opposite directions.
  • Practice by rewriting the same sentence three ways. Seeing how the meaning shifts with each of the three words builds the instinct faster than memorizing definitions.

The Bottom Line

Must, have to, and should aren't difficult because they're obscure — they're difficult because they all get taught as synonyms for "obligation" when they actually come from three different sources: personal conviction, external rule, and simple advice. Once you start asking where the obligation is coming from before picking a word, this becomes one of the more consistent, predictable scoring areas in the grammar section.

Q1 (Common Exam Pattern): Find the error: "All candidates must to bring a valid photo ID to the exam center."

Answer: "must to bring" → "must bring"

Explanation: Like all modals, "must" connects directly to the base form of the verb, with no "to" in between. The correct sentence is: "All candidates must bring a valid photo ID to the exam center."

Answer: have to

Explanation: "As per company policy" signals an external rule rather than the speaker's personal opinion, which pairs with "have to" rather than "must."

Q3 (Common Exam Pattern): Find the error: "You must not worry about carrying extra pens, they will be provided at the center."

Answer: "must not worry" → "don't have to worry"

Explanation: The sentence describes something optional (pens will be provided), not something forbidden. "Must not" wrongly suggests prohibition, while "don't have to" correctly signals that the action isn't required.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there really a difference between "must" and "have to," or are they the same?

They're related but not identical. "Must" reflects the speaker's own judgment, while "have to" reflects an obligation coming from an outside rule or authority.

Why does "have to" change form when "must" doesn't?

"Have to" behaves like a regular verb phrase and changes with subject and tense ("has to," "had to"), while "must" is a true modal and stays the same in every form.

What's the real difference between "must not" and "don't have to"?

"Must not" means an action is forbidden. "Don't have to" means an action is optional — it's still allowed, just not required.

Is "should" ever as strong as "must"?

No. "Should" always expresses advice or recommendation, while "must" expresses a firm requirement or strong personal conviction. They aren't interchangeable in formal English.

How do I decide between "must" and "have to" quickly during an exam?

Ask whether the obligation comes from the speaker's own opinion (must) or from an external rule, law, or authority (have to). This one question resolves most cases.
