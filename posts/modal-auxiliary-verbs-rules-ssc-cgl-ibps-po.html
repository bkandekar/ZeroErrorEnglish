In SSC CGL, IBPS PO and Railway exams, modal auxiliary verbs quietly steal 2–3 marks from almost every paper. Error spotting, sentence improvement and cloze tests love to place can, could, may, might, must, should, ought to, need and dare in positions that look correct but break the exact rule the examiner wants. Textbooks list the meanings; they never show you the exam traps. This post treats modals as one connected system and gives you the classroom method that converts those traps into easy marks.

Quick Answer

Modal auxiliaries never change form for person or number, never take “to” after them (except ought to and used to), and never take the –ing or –ed form of the main verb. In exams the single most tested rule is the correct pairing of modal + bare infinitive and the difference between possibility, ability, permission, obligation and deduction. Master the signal words and the five core patterns and you stop losing marks on this topic.

Why Modal Auxiliary Verbs Feel Harder Than They Are

Students treat every modal as a separate vocabulary item. That approach collapses the moment the paper mixes ability, permission, obligation and logical deduction in the same set of questions. In reality, modals form one tight system: each one signals a specific degree of possibility, necessity or permission, and each follows the same structural rules. Once you see the system, the individual words stop confusing you.

  • Error-spotting questions hide the wrong form of the main verb after a modal (can going, must to go, should went).
  • Sentence-improvement options change only the modal while the rest of the sentence stays identical, so you must know exact meaning differences.
  • Cloze tests place “can / could / may / might” in contexts of past ability, polite permission or weak possibility and expect the precise choice.
  • Mixed-pattern sentences combine two modals or a modal with “have + V3” and students panic because textbooks never taught the perfect-modal structure.
  • Negative and interrogative forms of need and dare switch between modal and main-verb behaviour, creating two correct-looking but mutually exclusive options.

In twenty-two years of classroom teaching I have watched bright students lose marks on the same three patterns every single year. The problem is never lack of intelligence; it is the absence of a single connected map of how modals actually work in exam English.

What most people get wrong is treating “can” and “could”, “may” and “might”, “must” and “have to” as interchangeable synonyms. They are not. Each carries a fixed signal that the examiner tests. Once you lock those signals, the questions become mechanical.

Common Mistakes Students Make

1. Using “to” after every modal
Students write “He can to swim” or “She must to finish” because they remember “want to”, “need to” and “ought to”. Only “ought to” and “used to” take “to”. All pure modals take the bare infinitive.

2. Changing the form of the modal for third-person singular
You will see “He cans drive” or “She musts go” in error-spotting questions. Modals never take –s, –ed or –ing. The form stays fixed for every subject.

3. Confusing perfect modals with simple past
“He must go yesterday” looks natural to many students, yet the correct structure for past deduction or past obligation is “must have + V3” or “should have + V3”. Missing the “have + past participle” costs the mark every time.

✔ Correct: He must finish the work today.

The Complete Modal System for Exams

Think of modals as a single family that follows five unbreakable structural rules and then divides into clear meaning groups. The table below gives you the exam-ready map.

Modal Core Exam Meaning Structure Typical Trap
can / could Ability, permission, possibility modal + bare V1 can to / could went
may / might Permission, weak possibility modal + bare V1 may be going (wrong when pure possibility needed)
must Strong obligation / logical deduction modal + bare V1 / must have + V3 must to / must went
should / ought to Advice, mild obligation, expectation should + bare V1 / ought to + V1 should to / ought + V1 (missing to)
need / dare Necessity / courage (modal or main verb) need/dare + bare V1 (modal) OR need/dare to + V1 mixing the two patterns in negatives
used to Past habit / past state used to + bare V1 use to / used to going

Step 1 — Lock the bare-infinitive rule
Every pure modal is followed by the base form of the verb. No –s, no –ed, no –ing, no “to” (except ought to and used to). This single rule kills more than half the error-spotting questions on modals.

Rule: Modal + bare infinitive
Exam example: She can speak three languages. (not can to speak / can speaking)

Step 2 — Separate ability, permission and possibility
“Can” and “could” cover ability and permission. “May” and “might” cover permission and weak possibility. When the sentence shows a real ability in the present, choose “can”. When the context is polite or uncertain, “could / may / might” become the safer options.

Step 3 — Master perfect modals for past reference
Past ability that was not used, past advice that was ignored, and past logical deduction all use the pattern modal + have + past participle. This structure appears regularly in both SSC and Bank exams.

Rule: Modal + have + V3 = past reference
Exam example: You should have informed me earlier. (not should informed / should have inform)

Step 4 — Treat “need” and “dare” as special cases
In affirmative sentences they usually behave as main verbs (need to / dare to). In negatives and questions they can drop “to” and act as pure modals. The exam loves to place both patterns side by side.

Step 5 — Watch the obligation scale
“Must” = strong internal obligation or certainty.
“Have to” = external obligation.
“Should / ought to” = advice or mild duty.
Mixing these three is a classic sentence-improvement trap.

Step 6 — Use signal words as your final check
Certain words almost force a particular modal. Learn the short list below and the choice becomes automatic.

Signal / Context Preferred Modal
ability in present can
past ability / polite request could
permission (formal) may
weak possibility might / may
strong obligation / certainty must
advice / mild duty should / ought to
past habit used to
past criticism / regret should have + V3
Step-by-Step Strategy
1 Identify the time reference — present, past or general.
2 Decide the meaning group — ability, permission, obligation, possibility or deduction.
3 Apply the bare-infinitive rule (or have + V3 for past).
4 Check for need / dare special behaviour in negatives and questions.
5 Eliminate options that break structure or change meaning.
6 Confirm with the signal-word list before final selection.

Expert Tips From the Classroom

  • Never add “to” after pure modals. The moment you see “can to / must to / should to”, mark it wrong without reading further.
  • Past reference almost always needs “have + V3”. Train your eye to look for the missing “have” in every should / must / could sentence that talks about yesterday.
  • In cloze tests, read one sentence before and after. The surrounding context usually locks the exact degree of possibility or obligation.
  • Treat “ought to” and “used to” as the only two exceptions that keep “to”. Everything else drops it.
  • When two options look identical, check the obligation scale. Must is stronger than should; have to is external while must is internal.

Practice Questions

Q1. Identify the error: She can to drive the car carefully in the night.

Answer: Error in “can to drive”. Correct: She can drive the car carefully in the night.

Explanation: Pure modals are followed by the bare infinitive. “To” is never used after can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would. This is the most frequent structural error tested in SSC and Bank exams.

Q2. Choose the correct option: You _______ have informed the manager about the delay yesterday.

(a) should    (b) should to    (c) should have    (d) must to

Answer: (c) should have

Explanation: The sentence refers to a past action that was desirable but did not happen. The required structure is modal + have + past participle. “Should have informed” is the only grammatically and meaningfully correct choice.

Q3. Find the correct sentence:

(a) He musts finish the project today.
(b) He must finish the project today.
(c) He must to finish the project today.
(d) He must finishing the project today.

Answer: (b) He must finish the project today.

Explanation: Modals never take –s for third person singular and never take “to” or the –ing form. Only the bare infinitive is correct after must.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Is “can” used for future ability?

In exam English “can” is mainly present ability or permission. For future ability the safer and more frequent choice is “will be able to”, though “can” appears in some informal contexts. Stick to “will be able to” when the option is available.

Q2. What is the difference between “must” and “have to” in exams?

“Must” shows strong internal obligation or logical certainty. “Have to” shows external obligation (rules, laws, circumstances). Error-spotting and sentence-improvement questions regularly test this distinction.

Q3. Can “need” and “dare” be used without “to”?

Yes, in negative and interrogative sentences they can behave as pure modals: “Need he go?” / “He need not go.” In affirmative sentences they usually take “to”. Both patterns are tested.

Q4. When do we use “should have + V3”?

When we talk about a desirable past action that did not happen, or when we express mild criticism or regret about the past. This structure is a favourite in both SSC and Bank papers.

Q5. Is “ought to” interchangeable with “should”?

In most exam contexts they are treated as near synonyms for advice or mild obligation. The only structural difference is that “ought” always keeps “to”. Never write “ought + bare verb”.
