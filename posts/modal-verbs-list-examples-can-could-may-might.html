Ask any student to explain the difference between "can" and "may," and you'll usually get the textbook answer: "can" is ability, "may" is permission. That's true, but it's also incomplete — and incomplete is exactly what costs marks in SSC CGL and Bank PO error-spotting sections. These four modals — can, could, may, might — get taught as a simple meaning chart in most classrooms, when in reality exams test the finer edges: when "could" is politeness rather than past ability, when "might" signals a weaker possibility than "may," and when swapping one for another quietly changes what a sentence actually means.

Quick Answer: "Can" and "could" relate to ability and informal permission (could being more polite or past-tense). "May" and "might" relate to possibility and formal permission (might being a weaker, less certain possibility than may). All four are followed directly by the base form of the verb, with no "to" and no change for person or number.

Why Can, Could, May, Might Feel Harder Than They Are

These four modals seem simple because they show up constantly in everyday English — "Can I ask something?", "It might rain." But that everyday comfort is exactly what causes trouble in an exam setting. Here's where students typically lose marks:

  • "Can" and "may" get treated as fully interchangeable for permission, when formal exam English still distinguishes them.
  • "Could" gets read only as "past of can," missing its very common use as a polite request in present time.
  • "May" and "might" are assumed to mean the same degree of possibility, when "might" is meant to sound less certain.
  • Negative forms are misread — "may not" can mean either "is not allowed to" or "possibly will not," and students often pick the wrong sense in context.
  • Students confidently use these modals in speech but freeze the moment a sentence-improvement question isolates just one of them.

After two decades of teaching this exact topic, one pattern repeats every batch: students don't struggle with meaning in isolation — they struggle with picking the right one between two options that both "sound correct." That's precisely the skill error-spotting and sentence-improvement questions are built to test.

Common Mistakes Students Make

Mistake 1: Using "can" in place of "may" in strictly formal writing. In everyday speech this barely matters, but formal or official-sounding exam sentences often expect "may" for permission — and questions are built around spotting that expectation.

Mistake 2: Treating "might" as identical to "may." "Might" expresses a noticeably weaker possibility than "may." Swapping them changes the confidence level of the sentence, which exams test directly in sentence-improvement questions.

Mistake 3: Adding "to" after these modals. Just like all modals, can, could, may, and might connect straight to the base form of the verb — never "to."

The Complete System (Step-by-Step)

Instead of memorizing four separate words, group them into two clean pairs — can/could for ability and informal permission, may/might for possibility and formal permission. Once the pairing clicks, choosing the right one in a sentence becomes far more automatic.

ModalCore MeaningExample
CanPresent ability, informal permissionShe can finish this paper in 40 minutes.
CouldPast ability, polite request, weaker possibilityCould you explain that rule again?
MayFormal permission, moderate possibilityMay I submit my answer sheet early?
MightWeaker possibility than may, tentative suggestionThe results might be declared next week.

Step 1: Fix the base-form rule first. Whichever of these four modals appears, the verb right after it stays in its base form — no "s," no "-ing," no "-ed," no "to."

Rule + Example: Modal + V1 — "She may attends the seminar" is wrong; "She may attend the seminar" is correct.

Step 2: Separate ability from permission within "can" and "could." "Can" and "could" cover both — context tells you which. "She can swim" is ability; "Can I sit here?" is permission.

Step 3: Use "could" for politeness, not just for the past. "Could you help me with this question?" is present-time and polite — it isn't describing a past ability at all. This is one of the more commonly misunderstood uses in exam contexts.

Step 4: Rank "may" above "might" on the certainty scale. "It may rain" sounds more likely than "It might rain." When a sentence needs to sound more tentative or unsure, "might" is the correct choice.

Rule + Example: "May" = more likely possibility; "Might" = less likely possibility. "He may join the training" sounds more probable than "He might join the training."

Step 5: Watch the negative forms carefully. "May not" usually signals "not allowed to," while in some contexts it can also signal possibility ("It may not rain today"). Read the full sentence before deciding which sense fits.

Step 6: Never stack two of these modals together. "Might could," "may can" — none of these exist in standard exam English, no matter how natural they may sound in casual speech.

Formal request or official permissionmay
Casual request among friends/colleaguescan
Polite request in present timecould
Confident possibilitymay

Step-by-Step Strategy

1

Identify which of the four modals is used before reading the rest of the sentence.

2

Check whether the sentence is about ability, permission, or possibility.

3

If it's permission, decide if the tone is formal (may) or informal (can).

4

If it's possibility, decide if the confidence level fits "may" or the weaker "might."

5

6

Scan for a second modal sitting right beside it — that combination is always wrong.

A Quick Turnaround Story

An SSC CGL aspirant in one of my batches kept mixing up "may" and "might" in sentence-improvement questions, always picking whichever one "felt natural" rather than checking the certainty level the sentence actually needed. Once she started asking a single question before answering — "is this sentence confident or unsure?" — her accuracy on these specific questions improved noticeably within the same week. The grammar itself hadn't changed; what changed was that she stopped guessing by feel and started checking by rule.

Expert Tips From the Classroom

  • Read "could" as two separate tools, not one. Sometimes it's the past of "can," and sometimes it's pure politeness in the present — check the timeframe of the sentence first.
  • Rank possibility words on a scale. Certain → will; likely → may; less certain → might. This scale helps you choose instantly instead of guessing.
  • In formal or official-sounding sentences, default to "may" over "can." Exams often lean formal, and this single habit avoids a common trap.
  • Never let "sounds fine in speech" be your final answer. Spoken English blends these modals loosely; exam English does not.
  • Practice by rewriting the same sentence with all four modals. Seeing how the meaning shifts each time builds the instinct faster than memorizing definitions alone.

The Bottom Line

Can, could, may, and might aren't difficult because they're rare or unusual — they're difficult because they're so common that students stop questioning them. Once you treat "can/could" as the ability-and-informal-permission pair and "may/might" as the possibility-and-formal-permission pair, with "might" always sitting one notch below "may" in certainty, these four modals stop being a guessing game and start being one of the more predictable scoring areas in the grammar section.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there really a difference between "can" and "may" for permission?

Yes, in formal or exam-standard English. "May" is considered more correct for formal permission, while "can" is common in everyday informal speech.

Why is "could" sometimes used for the present instead of the past?

"Could" is commonly used as a polite way to make a request in present time, such as "Could you help me?" — this is separate from its role as the past tense of "can."

What's the actual difference between "may" and "might"?

Both express possibility, but "might" expresses a weaker, less certain possibility than "may." Exams often test this exact shade of difference in sentence-improvement questions.

Can "may not" mean two different things?

Yes. Depending on context, "may not" can mean "is not allowed to" (permission) or "possibly will not" (possibility). Reading the full sentence is the only way to tell which sense applies.

Do "can," "could," "may," and "might" ever take "to" after them?

No. Like all standard modals except "ought to," these four connect directly to the base form of the verb without "to."
