In SSC CGL, Bank PO and Railway papers, one wrong modal of probability can cost you a full mark in Error Spotting or Sentence Improvement. Textbooks neatly list “may / might / could / must / can’t,” but examiners deliberately mix certainty levels, time references and negative forms so the “correct” option looks wrong. This lesson treats modals of probability and possibility as one connected system — exactly the way the paper setters do — so you stop losing marks on patterns you already “know.”

Quick Answer

Modals of probability and possibility show how sure the speaker is about a present or past situation. In exams the key distinctions are: must (almost certain), may / might / could (possible), and can’t / couldn’t (almost impossible). Examiners test whether the modal matches the evidence in the sentence, whether the time reference is right (present vs past), and whether the negative form carries the correct logical force.

Why Modals of Probability Feel Harder Than They Are

Most students memorise a short list: must = certainty, may/might = possibility. Then they walk into the exam hall and freeze the moment a sentence mixes present evidence with a past action, or when the options offer both “might have” and “must have.” The topic feels harder than it is because textbooks teach isolated rules while examiners test three things at the same time — degree of certainty, time reference, and the logical force of the negative.

  • You see “must” and immediately think of obligation, forgetting that in probability contexts it simply means “almost certain from the evidence.”
  • You treat “may” and “might” as free substitutes, so you miss the slight preference for “might” when the possibility is weaker or more hypothetical.
  • You ignore the time signal (“yesterday,” “already,” “still”) and pick a present modal for a past deduction.
  • You reverse the force of “can’t,” treating it as weak possibility instead of strong impossibility.
  • You never practise the mixed-pattern sentences that appear in cloze tests and sentence improvement, where the modal has to fit both grammar and logic.

In my classroom I have watched the same pattern for years. A student who can recite the rules still loses marks because the question is not asking “What does must mean?” It is asking “Given this evidence, which modal correctly shows the speaker’s certainty?” That is the gap most people never close.

What most aspirants get wrong is treating these modals as vocabulary items. They are logical operators. Once you start reading the evidence in the sentence first and only then choosing the modal, the topic becomes one of the most predictable scoring areas in the paper.

Common Mistakes Students Make

1. Confusing obligation “must” with probability “must”
Students see “must” and automatically think of duty or compulsion. In probability questions the same word signals near-certainty based on evidence. Choosing the obligation meaning leads straight to wrong options in Error Spotting.

2. Treating “may / might / could” as completely free substitutes
All three express possibility, but examiners often prefer “might” or “could” when the chance is remote or hypothetical, and “may” when it is more open. Ignoring that shade costs marks in Sentence Improvement.

3. Missing the past-time deduction form
A sentence about a past event needs “must have + V3,” “might have + V3” or “can’t have + V3.” Students keep using the present form and lose the mark even though they understood the meaning.

Common Mistakes Students Make

❌ Wrong: He is not in his office. He must go home early.
✅ Correct: He is not in his office. He must have gone home early.
Reason: The absence is present evidence about a past action → past deduction form is required.

❌ Wrong: The lights are off. They might be at home.
✅ Correct: The lights are off. They can’t be at home. / They must be out.
Reason: Lights off is strong evidence against their being at home; “might” is too weak.

❌ Wrong: She may has finished the work by now.
✅ Correct: She may have finished the work by now.
Reason: After a modal the perfect infinitive is “have + V3,” never “has.”

The Solution — One Connected System

Stop learning five separate rules. Learn one decision path that covers every exam question on probability and possibility modals.

Modal Degree of Certainty Typical Exam Use Time Form
must Almost certain (≈95%) Strong positive deduction from evidence must + V1 (present) / must have + V3 (past)
can’t / couldn’t Almost impossible (≈5%) Strong negative deduction can’t + V1 / can’t have + V3
may Possible (open possibility) Neutral or slightly optimistic possibility may + V1 / may have + V3
might Possible but weaker / more remote Hypothetical or less likely possibility might + V1 / might have + V3
could Possible (often theoretical) Ability-based or theoretical possibility; also past of can could + V1 / could have + V3

Step-by-Step Decision Path

Step 1 — Read the evidence first
Before looking at the options, underline the factual clue in the sentence (lights off, empty chair, wet roads, exam result, etc.). The modal must match the strength of that evidence.

Rule: Strong clear evidence → must / can’t. Weak or incomplete evidence → may / might / could.
Example: The road is completely dry. It can’t have rained last night.

Step 2 — Decide present or past deduction
If the situation is current, use modal + base verb. If the situation is about a finished past action, use modal + have + past participle.

Step 3 — Match the force of the negative
“Can’t” is the opposite of “must.” It is not a weak “may not.” Choosing “may not” when the evidence makes the action almost impossible is a classic error-spotting trap.

Step 4 — Check subject–verb agreement after the modal
The verb after a modal is always the base form. “She must goes” or “He may has” is always wrong.

Step 5 — Watch for “could” in two roles
In probability questions “could” often means theoretical possibility. In other contexts it is simply the past of “can.” Context decides.

Step 6 — Eliminate options that mix obligation with probability
If the sentence is clearly about deduction from evidence, discard any option that forces an obligation reading.

Signal / Marker Preferred Modal Family
Clear present evidence (empty desk, no reply, lights off) must / can’t
Incomplete or soft evidence may / might / could
Past time marker + present evidence must have / might have / can’t have
Hypothetical or remote chance might / could
Strong denial of possibility can’t / couldn’t

Step-by-Step Strategy

1 Underline the factual evidence in the sentence.
2 Decide whether the deduction is about the present or the past.
3 Choose the certainty level that matches the strength of the evidence.
4 Apply the correct form (base verb or have + V3).
5 Eliminate obligation readings and subject–verb agreement errors.
6 Confirm that the negative form carries the right logical force.

A Quick Turnaround Story

One of my Bank PO batch students kept marking “may have” in every past-possibility question. After three consecutive mock tests he still scored zero in that slot. We spent one session only on evidence strength: I gave him ten sentences and asked him to rate the evidence as “strong” or “weak” before choosing the modal. The next mock he got all five probability-modal questions correct. The change was not new rules; it was a new reading habit — evidence first, modal second.

Expert Tips From the Classroom

  • Always mark the evidence first. If the paper gives clear proof, “must” or “can’t” is almost always safer than “may” or “might.”
  • Train the perfect infinitive separately. Write twenty sentences of the pattern “must / might / can’t have + V3” until the form becomes automatic.
  • Never equate “can’t” with “may not.” In exam logic “can’t” is the strong opposite of “must.”
  • In cloze tests, look at the surrounding certainty. A paragraph that builds strong evidence will reject a weak modal even if the grammar looks fine.
  • Practise mixed-pattern sentences. The real exam rarely gives a pure textbook example; it mixes time, polarity and certainty in one line.

The Bottom Line

Modals of probability and possibility stop being a weak area the moment you treat them as a single decision system instead of five separate vocabulary items. Read the evidence, decide the time frame, match the certainty level, and apply the correct form. That sequence is what examiners reward — and what textbooks almost never teach as a connected process.

Practice Questions

Q1. The lights are still on in her cabin. She ________ the office yet.

(a) must leave    (b) must have left    (c) can’t have left    (d) may leave

**Summary:** Click to view step-by-step solution & rule

Answer: (c) can’t have left

Explanation: Present evidence (lights still on) strongly suggests she has not left. The action is past relative to now, so we need the perfect form. “Can’t have left” correctly expresses strong impossibility of the past action. “Must have left” would contradict the evidence.

Q2. He looks exhausted. He ________ the entire night working on the project.

(a) must spend    (b) must have spent    (c) might spend    (d) can spend

Answer: (b) must have spent

Explanation: Visible present result (exhaustion) leads to a strong deduction about a past action. Hence “must have + V3.” Options with present forms ignore the time reference of the completed night’s work.

Q3. I’m not sure of the final figures, but the company ________ a small profit this quarter.

(a) must make    (b) must have made    (c) might have made    (d) can’t make

Answer: (c) might have made

Explanation: The phrase “I’m not sure” signals weak evidence. A strong modal like “must” is therefore inappropriate. “Might have made” correctly shows a tentative past possibility.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the difference between “may” and “might” in probability questions?

Both express possibility. “May” is slightly more open or neutral; “might” often suggests a more remote or hypothetical chance. In many exam items either is accepted, but when options force a choice, prefer “might” for weaker or more tentative situations.

Q. When do we use “must have” instead of “must”?

Use “must have + past participle” when the deduction concerns a past action or state. Use “must + base verb” for a present situation.

Q. Is “can’t” the same as “may not”?

No. “Can’t” expresses near-certainty that something is impossible. “May not” only says it is possible that something does not happen. Examiners treat them as different degrees of force.

Q. Can “could” be used for present possibility?

Yes. In probability contexts “could” often means theoretical or open possibility in the present or future, as well as past possibility with “could have.”

Q. How should I approach these questions in a cloze test?

Read the whole paragraph for the level of certainty the writer is building. A paragraph that presents strong evidence will reject a weak modal even if the single sentence looks grammatically acceptable.
